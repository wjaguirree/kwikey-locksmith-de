Can a Locksmith Copy a Do Not Duplicate Key?

Can a locksmith copy a Do Not Duplicate key? Learn what the stamp really means, which keys are truly restricted, and what your options are in Wilmington, DE.

October 26, 2026 Kwikey Locksmith
Not sure if your key can be copied? Call us with the key's markings and we'll tell you what we can do. (302) 551-2550

Usually, yes. A "Do Not Duplicate" stamp on a key is a request, not a lock. Any shop with the right blank and a cutting machine can copy it, because the stamp has no legal force behind it. The keys that truly can't be copied by just anyone are restricted keys, and those are protected by patents and dealer rules instead of by words printed on the brass.

Here's how to tell the two apart, what to do if you're a tenant or a property manager here in Wilmington, DE, and when it makes more sense to change the lock than to copy the key.

Key Takeaways

  • The stamp is a warning. It doesn't stop a blade from cutting.
  • Restricted keys are different, and only authorized dealers can cut them.
  • If you need control over who holds a key, change the lock system instead of trusting the stamp.
  • Not sure what you're holding? Call us before you make a trip.

What does "Do Not Duplicate" mean on a key?

It means somebody wanted the key to stay a one-of-a-kind. A property manager, a school, a business owner or a past homeowner ordered key blanks with the words already stamped in, hoping that would make people think twice.

Think of it like a "Please Do Not Touch" sign in a museum. It asks nicely. It doesn't build a wall. The key still has grooves along the blade, and a cutting machine reads those grooves just fine.

Is it illegal to copy a Do Not Duplicate key?

No, not as a rule. Medeco, a high-security lock maker, says plainly that there is no legal weight behind a key marked "do not duplicate" and that no criminal statutes cover it (Medeco). ASSA ABLOY says much the same, noting that typically no legal statutes or penalties back up a key stamped with those words (ASSA ABLOY).

That said, a shop can still say no. Some locksmiths and hardware stores won't touch a stamped key, because they'd rather not get in the middle of a dispute with whoever owns the lock. So the honest answer is that it's legal, it's usually possible, and it depends on the shop you walk into.

How do you tell a stamped key from a restricted key?

This is the part most people miss. Two keys can look just as official and still behave totally differently.

Stamped key Restricted key
What it says "Do Not Duplicate" or "Do Not Copy" Often a brand name or a serial number
What stops copying Nothing but a request A patent, a special keyway and a dealer agreement
Can any shop copy it Usually, yes No, only authorized dealers
Typical use Older apartments, offices, schools High-security homes and commercial sites
Blank available to the public Yes No

The strength of a restricted key comes from the blank, not the print. If a shop can't buy the blank, it can't cut the key. Patent protection is the reason. A U.S. utility patent can last up to 20 years from the date the application was filed (USPTO), which is a long time to keep a keyway to yourself.

Our commercial and residential work includes high-security locks from makers like Medeco and Mul-T-Lock for exactly this reason. They're built so that key copying stays under the owner's control.

The 3-Check Key Test

Before you spend a day chasing a copy, run through this quick routine. We call it the 3-Check Key Test.

  1. Check the key. Look for a brand name, a patent mark or a number on the blade or bow. A plain stamp with only the words "Do Not Duplicate" and nothing else is almost always an ordinary key.
  2. Check the lock. Look at the face of the cylinder for a maker's name. A recognizable high-security brand is a strong sign the key is restricted.
  3. Check the owner. Ask who put the lock in. If it's a landlord, a business or an HOA, they may hold the authorization, and a letter from them can save you a lot of back and forth.

Two ordinary results and one brand name? You're probably fine. A brand name on both the key and the lock? Expect to go through the owner or an authorized dealer.

What if you rent or manage the property?

Renters have a tricky spot here. Your lease may say you can't copy keys, even though no law stops the machine. Copying one anyway can put you on the wrong side of your landlord, so ask first. A short message to the property manager usually gets you a yes, or at least a clear answer on who handles extra keys.

Managers and business owners face the reverse problem. If you hand out keys to staff, contractors or tenants, a stamp won't protect you. What does work is a master key system with a restricted keyway, so you decide who can get a copy and you can track the keys you've issued. It's one of the more reliable ways to keep key control with the person who owns the building.

Hold on to the paperwork. If your locks ever get rekeyed or replaced, written records of who holds which key make the next step easy.

What about car keys?

Car keys are their own world. A modern key often carries a transponder chip, so cutting the metal isn't enough. The car also has to recognize the chip. A stamp on the plastic doesn't change that, and neither does the lack of one.

If you're after a spare for your vehicle, our car key duplication work is done where the car is parked, and our guide to transponder keys explains why a plain copy from a hardware store may start nothing at all. Our earlier piece on how transponder keys work goes deeper.

When should you rekey instead of copying?

Sometimes the better question isn't "Can I copy this key?" It's "Do I know who already has one?"

If the key came with a house you just bought, a rental turnover, or a business where staff have come and gone, you can't know how many copies are out there. A stamp tells you nothing about that. Rekeying changes the inside of the lock so old keys stop working, and you get fresh ones you control.

Here's a quick way to decide.

Your situation Better move
You need one spare for your own lock Copy the key
You can't say who else has a key Rekey the lock
You moved in recently Rekey, then copy the new key
Staff or tenants changed Rekey or move to a restricted system
The key is stamped but plain Ask a shop whether it will cut it

Frequently asked questions

Can a locksmith copy a Do Not Duplicate key?

Often, yes. The stamp has no legal force, so a shop with the matching blank can usually cut it. Some shops choose not to, so it helps to ask first.

Is it illegal to copy a key that says Do Not Copy?

Generally not. Lock makers like Medeco and ASSA ABLOY both say no law backs up the wording. The exception is a patented restricted key, which only authorized dealers can cut.

Why do some places refuse to copy my key?

They may be following their own store policy, or they don't want to be caught between you and the property owner. Restricted keys are different, since a shop that isn't an authorized dealer can't buy the blank at all.

How do I know if my key is restricted?

Look for a brand name, patent mark or serial number on the key, and check the lock for a high-security maker's name. When in doubt, call a locksmith with the markings in hand.

Can I copy a work key or an apartment key?

Check with your employer or landlord first. The law may not stop you, but your lease or workplace rules might.

Can car keys stamped Do Not Duplicate be copied?

Car keys are cut and then programmed. The stamp doesn't matter much, but the transponder chip does, since the vehicle has to accept the new key.
